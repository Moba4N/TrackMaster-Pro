    1Welcome

    TrackMaster Pro is a planning program for model railway layouts. You draw your layout on screen before you buy the first rail or saw the first batten: baseboard, track, gradients, scenery, power and signals. The program works out the dimensions for you, warns you about mistakes and shows the result in 3D.

    What TrackMaster Pro can do

    You do not have to use everything at once. For a first small layout, the baseboard, the track and the plan check are enough. Everything else can come later, when you need it.

    2Installation

    You only need one of the three versions. The web version runs without installation; you install the Windows program and the Android app once.

    Requirements

    • Windows 10 or Windows 11, 64-bit
    • about 400 MB of free disk space
    • screen of at least 1280 × 720 pixels, 1920 × 1080 recommended
    • for the 3D view, a graphics card with DirectX support (present in every current PC)

    You do not need to install anything else. Everything TrackMaster Pro needs is included in the installation package.

    Installing

    1. Download the file TrackMasterPro-1.5.0.msi. It usually ends up in the Downloads folder.
    2. Double-click the file.
    3. If Windows reports “Windows protected your PC”, click More info and then Run anyway. This message appears for programs that Windows has not seen often yet.
    4. The setup wizard welcomes you. Click Next.
    5. Read the licence terms, tick I accept the terms and click Next.
    6. Keep the suggested destination folder (C:\Program Files\TrackMaster Pro) and click Next and Install. Windows asks whether the program may make changes. Confirm with Yes.
    7. After a few seconds everything is done. Click Finish.

    You will then find TrackMaster Pro in the Start menu and as a shortcut on the desktop. This manual is also in the Start menu.

To remove the program, open Settings › Apps › Installed apps in Windows, find TrackMaster Pro and click Uninstall. Your plan files and the licence file are not deleted.

    3Licence and activation

    The Pro edition is unlocked with a personal licence file. The file is digitally signed and cannot be altered. The program checks it every time it starts.

    Installing the licence

    1. Save the licence file you received after purchase (extension .lic) on the computer, for example in the Downloads folder.
    2. Open File › Licence…. On Windows you can also use Home › Licence.
    3. Click Install licence… and select the file. In the web version you can also paste the file's content into the text box.
    4. The window now shows Pro, the licensee's name and, if present, the expiry date. The indicator at the top or in the status bar changes from “Basic” to “Pro”.

    On Windows the licence is then stored in your user data (%APPDATA%\TrackMaster Pro\trackmaster.lic) and applies at every start. On Android you install the licence the same way via File › Licence… and choose the file from the device's storage.

    Online activation: up to two devices

    A licence without computer binding may run on two devices at the same time, for example on the PC at home and on a tablet. Every device counts, whether Windows, Android or browser. For this to work, the device registers once over the internet the first time it starts with the licence. After that it checks about once a week whether the registration still exists. The licence window shows the status in the Activation section.

    • Without internet, Pro keeps running for up to 14 days after installing the licence. A device that has been activated stays activated without internet.
    • If both places are taken, the third device stays on the Basic edition. The message names the registered devices.
    • Changing devices: on the old device choose Release this device in the licence window. Then click Activate now on the new device. If the old device no longer exists (broken, sold), the publisher frees the place on request.
    • Browser: every browser counts as a separate device. If you delete the site data, it registers as a new device next time.

    Licence bound to a computer

    A licence can be bound to a particular computer or device. For this the publisher needs the computer ID (on Android: device ID). It is shown in the licence window. Copy puts it on the clipboard; then paste it into your e-mail (Ctrl+V).

    If you choose a Pro function in the Basic edition

    If you choose the parts list, printing or an export, or save a plan with more than 50 tracks, a note appears. Licence… takes you straight to the licence window. Your plan stays open and unchanged.

    CautionIn the Basic edition, plans with more than 50 tracks cannot be saved. If you then close the program, start a new plan or open another one, TrackMaster Pro asks whether the changes should be discarded. Only answer Yes if you really no longer need the plan.

    4First steps for beginners

    Have you never planned a model railway on a computer before? Then this chapter is for you. It explains the most important words and how to use the mouse and keyboard, and takes you step by step to your first small layout. You need no previous knowledge and no licence.

    The most important terms

    A track plan is a drawing of your layout seen from above, to the correct scale. It consists of individual track pieces, just as the real layout is made of track you buy. You will meet these words all the time:

    TermWhat it means
    GaugeThe size of the model railway. The most common is H0 (scale 1:87). N is smaller (1:160), Z very small (1:220), G large, for the garden. You choose the gauge at the start. It determines which track is available.
    ScaleHow much smaller the model is than the real thing. At 1:87, 1 cm on the model is 87 cm in reality.
    Track systemA manufacturer's track range. Pieces of one system fit together exactly; pieces of different systems usually need an adapter. Choose the system you want to buy or already own.
    Track pieceA single part: straight, curve, turnout (where the track divides), crossing (two tracks cross) or buffer stop (the end of a siding).
    Track endEvery track piece has ends where the next one connects. A straight has two, a turnout three. An end without a connection is an open track end and is marked orange in the plan.
    Active track endThe open end to which the program attaches the next track. You choose it by clicking an orange end.
    RadiusHow tight a curve is, measured from the centre of the circle to the middle of the track. Small radii save space; large ones look better, and long coaches run more safely on them.
    BaseboardThe surface the layout is built on, often a wooden board, for example 2 × 1 m.
    MillimetresAll dimensions in the program are millimetres: 1 m = 1000 mm, 1 cm = 10 mm. A 2 × 1 m board is therefore 2000 × 1000 mm.

    More words are explained in the Glossary at the end of the manual.

    Your first layout in 10 steps

    We build a simple oval with a siding on a 2 × 1 m board in H0. It takes about a quarter of an hour. An oval consists of two semicircles of curves, joined by straight pieces. The train runs round and round on it.

    1. Create a new plan. Click File and then New (or press Ctrl+N). The New plan window opens.
    2. Choose gauge and baseboard. Under Gauge choose H0 and as the shape Rectangle. Enter 2000 as the width and 1000 as the depth (millimetres!). Leave Construction at Plan directly on level 1. Click Create plan (web version: Create). An empty baseboard now lies on the drawing area.
    3. Choose a track system. At the top of the track library choose your track system under Manufacturer / track system. The list below shows all pieces with article number and size. The buttons Straight, Curves, Turnouts etc. show only that kind.
    4. Place the first curve. Click Curves and then once on a curve with a small radius. It appears on the baseboard. Its free end is orange: that is where the next track goes.
    5. Complete the semicircle. Keep clicking the same curve. Each click adds a piece to the orange end. A semicircle is 180°. With 30° curves you therefore need six pieces; you can see the angle next to the track in the library. It is quicker with the Enter key: it adds the last chosen track again each time.
    6. Add straight pieces. Click Straight and three or four times on the same straight. The line continues straight ahead. Remember how many straights you used.
    7. Build the second half with a turnout. Add six curves again. Then click Turnouts and once on a turnout. It is added like a straight piece; its branch stays open as an orange end. Now, if necessary, click the orange end that continues straight on, and add straights until you are back at the start. If the ends meet exactly, the program joins them by itself. If a small gap remains, click one of the two ends and then Tracks › Close gap: the program inserts a matching piece of flexible track.
    8. Lay the siding. Click the orange end at the branch of the turnout. Add two or three straights and finally, under Other, a buffer stop. If the branch points the wrong way, for example off the baseboard, select the turnout and press F (mirror). The status bar at the bottom then shows open ends 0.
    9. Check. Press F7 (on Windows Home › Collision check, in the web version Home › Plan check). The list reports whether tracks touch, stick out over the edge or have open ends. Clicking an entry shows the spot. If nothing is listed, everything is fine.
    10. Look and save. Click 3D › 3D view and turn the layout with the left mouse button held down. Close the window and save with Ctrl+S. Enter a file name, for example “My first layout”. Done!
    TipIf a track faces the wrong way, press A (switch connection) or F (mirror) straight afterwards. This turns the last placed track around. If a track lies off to the side, drag it with the mouse to an orange end: it snaps into place by itself.
    Example: Does the oval fit on the board?A curve with a 360 mm radius needs 2 × 360 = 720 mm of depth for a semicircle, plus about the width of the track. On a board 1000 mm deep that leaves roughly 120 mm of margin at the top and bottom. With a 437 mm radius curve it would be 874 mm plus track, which is already tight. The plan check reports it if a track sticks out over the edge.

    6Creating a plan and adding track

    This chapter sums up the steps every plan starts with: creating a new plan, choosing the baseboard and track system, adding track. A detailed guide for your very first plan is in chapter 4.

    Creating a new plan

    1. Choose File › New (Ctrl+N). A plan that has not been saved is discarded; Ctrl+Z brings it back.
    2. Choose the gauge, for example H0.
    3. Choose the baseboard shape and enter the dimensions. The preview shows the shape immediately.
    4. Choose the construction (see below). For a start, Plan directly on level 1 is right.
    5. Click Create plan (web version: Create).
    6. Click an empty spot next to the baseboard and enter a name under Plan name in the properties on the right. It appears on the printout and is suggested as the file name when saving.
    Baseboard shapeDimensions and special features
    RectangleWidth and depth. The simplest and most common shape.
    L shapeOuter dimensions and leg depth, orientation in 90° steps. Good for a room corner.
    U shapeOuter dimensions and leg depth; the two legs may be of different length. Good for three walls, with room to stand in the middle.
    T and Z shapeOuter dimensions, leg depth and orientation.
    E and G shapeOuter dimensions, leg depth and orientation. The E shape has three legs, the G shape an inward hook.
    RingLayout all round with an open centre, for example with an access point in the middle.
    ModulesLayout made of individual rectangular parts. Width and depth apply to the first module at first (chapter 7).

    Construction. This decides whether a wooden frame goes under the baseboard right away:

    • Plan directly on level 1: only the baseboard. You can add benchwork later at any time.
    • Benchwork from a template: the plan gets a wooden frame immediately. You can choose Frame with top board (the usual layout table), Open frame (rib construction) without a top board, L-girder frame for large free-standing layouts and Exhibition module (1,300 mm high).
    • Draw benchwork yourself: only the baseboard is created and the Scenery › Draw batten tool is switched on straight away. You then draw the side rails, cross members, diagonals and braces yourself.

    You can change the shape and dimensions later at any time: click an empty spot or Scenery › Baseboard; the properties then show the baseboard dimensions. More about baseboard, modules and benchwork is in chapter 7.

    Choosing a track system

    You choose the gauge in the new plan or later on the Home tab at the far right in the Track gauge group. You choose the track system at the top of the track library under Manufacturer / track system. The library then shows all pieces of that system with article number and size. You may also mix several systems in one plan, for example for a siding made of leftover stock; you then have to check yourself whether the transitions fit in reality.

    Note on dimensionsThe track geometry follows the catalogue data. A few track systems are stored with provisional dimensions and are marked in the library. Check these against the real track before building.

    Adding track

    The basic principle is always the same: there is an active track end (orange), and every track you click in the library is added there.

    1. Click a track in the library. If the plan is still empty, it appears in the middle of the baseboard. Its free end is marked orange.
    2. Click more tracks. Each new track is added at the active end. Enter or the Space bar adds the last chosen track again.
    3. To continue somewhere else, click an orange end there. The E key jumps to the next open end.
    4. If an end lands exactly on another, TrackMaster Pro joins the two by itself. An oval closes this way without any action from you.

    Instead of clicking, you can also drag a track from the library onto the baseboard. Release it near an open end and it snaps into place there.

    Tip: turnout the wrong way round?A (switch connector) places the last track with its next connector at the same spot. This turns a turnout that splits the line into one that joins two tracks again. F (mirror) turns a left turnout into a right turnout, or makes a curve bend the other way.

    Checking the plan

    2. Put the mouse on an open track end and drag. The flexible track runs out of the track without a kink and bends as you move.
    3. When you get close to another track end, it snaps. The flexible track is then bent so that it joins both ends without a kink.
    4. Release the mouse button. Length and smallest radius are shown while you drag.

    CautionWhen dragging, watch the smallest radius shown. It should not be smaller than the smallest curve radius your vehicles can manage. If your locomotive's instructions say nothing about it, the smallest curve of your track system is a good guide.

    9Heights, levels, helix and rib construction

    Many layouts have more than one storey: a line crosses a bridge, a station is hidden under the scenery, or the railway climbs a mountain. This chapter explains how to raise track, build ramps, organise levels and have the timber parts for the benchwork calculated.

    How is height measured?All heights in the program are the height of the rail top above the baseboard, in millimetres. A track lying directly on the baseboard therefore has height 0. If it lies on a 10 mm cork strip, it is still 0 – you only enter something when it lies higher than the other tracks.

    Height of a track

    Every track has a height at the start and a gradient in percent. The program calculates the height at the other end itself. To change a track's height:

    1. Click the track. Its Properties appear on the right.
    2. Enter the height you want under Rail top at start (mm), for example 80.
    3. If the track is to stay level, leave the gradient at 0 %.

    If several tracks are selected, the height field puts all of them level at this height. With View › Heights the heights are written at the track ends in the plan. The program always draws higher lines over lower ones, so you can easily see bridges and underpasses.

    Gradient over a run of track

    A ramp is a stretch of line that rises evenly. Instead of calculating every track yourself, let the program spread the gradient.

    1. Select all tracks of the ramp: draw a frame around them with the mouse, or hold down Shift and click the tracks one after another.
    2. Click Tracks › Gradient….
    3. Enter the start height, that is, the height at the bottom of the ramp.
    4. Under Input, choose what you know:
      • End height – you know how high the ramp must arrive at the top. The program calculates the gradient.
      • Gradient in percent – you want a particular gradient. The program calculates the end height.
    5. If the ramp rises in the wrong direction, reverse the direction. Length, gradient and end height are shown below the fields. Confirm.
    Example: How long must the ramp be?One line is to cross over another. In H0 you need about 80 mm of height for this. At a 2.5 % gradient the track rises 25 mm per metre. For 80 mm you therefore need 80 ÷ 25 = 3.2 m of ramp. At 3 % it would be 2.7 m. Ramps are almost always longer than you think – plan them early.

    If two parallel runs of track are selected, for example a double-track ramp, the inner, shorter track gets exactly the gradient entered. The outer one rises so that it arrives at the same height.

    GradientAssessment
    up to 2 %very good, as on the real railway; every train gets up
    2 to 3 %usual on model railway layouts
    over 3 %the plan check warns; long or heavy trains need a second locomotive
    over 5 %the plan check reports an error; most locomotives slip here

    Height at a joint (web version only): double-click the point where two tracks meet – or right-click and Height at joint… – to change the height exactly there. Both tracks get the new height at this point, their other ends stay where they are, and the gradient adjusts. Turnouts and crossings are raised as a whole. With tracks selected, the heights at the joints are shown in the plan (↕ in mm).

    Height profile

    The height profile is a small chart that shows how the line goes up and down – like the profile of a mountain stage in a cycle race. Select connected tracks, for example a ramp or the whole line between two stations, and click Tracks › Height profile. The horizontal axis is the distance travelled, the vertical axis the height. Red parts are steeper than recommended; lengthen the ramp there.

    Levels

    Think of levels as the storeys of a house: at the bottom there may be a hidden fiddle yard (a storage yard under the layout where trains wait), above it the visible main level and at the top a mountain line. Every level has a name and a base height. Every track and every scenery item belongs to exactly one level. A layout can have up to 25 levels.

    The big advantage: you can hide a level. Then you see, for example, only the fiddle yard and can work there without the tracks above getting in the way.

    The level bar

    The level bar is at the bottom of the window. It has a button for each level.

    • The tick next to the name shows or hides the level.
    • Clicking the name makes the level active. New tracks always go onto the active level and start at its base height.
    • + adds a new level above.
    • Hide all hides all levels with one click; the same button is then called Show all and brings them back. Handy if you then want to switch just one level back on.
    • … opens the Levels window.

    The Levels window

    Tracks › Levels… (or View › Levels…) opens a list of all levels. There you can

    • add and rename levels and change their base height,
    • remove a level – its tracks move to the level below, nothing is lost,
    • show Only this level or bring all back with Show all,
    • move selected tracks to another level with Selected to active level. Their height changes by the difference between the two levels.

    If you change a level's base height, all its tracks move with it. Hills of a level stand on its base height, so the terrain adapts by itself.

    ImportantHidden levels are only missing from the display (plan and 3D view). They still count in the parts list and the plan check. If tracks have “suddenly disappeared”, their level is almost always hidden.

    Helix

    A helix is a line that winds upwards in a circle like a spiral staircase. It overcomes large height differences in little space – typically to connect a fiddle yard under the layout with the main level. The program builds it from curved track of your track system: round or oval, single-track or double-track.

    1. Click a free track end if the helix is to connect there. Without a selected end it is created in the middle of the view.
    2. Click Tracks › Helix….
    3. Choose the shape: Round or Oval. In an oval there are straight tracks on both long sides; choose the straight and how many per side.
    4. Choose single-track or double-track. With double track the second track is on the outside: as a curve with a matching radius or as flexible track with a freely chosen spacing.
    5. Choose the curve for the inner track, the number of turns, the height per turn and the direction (left- or right-handed).
    6. Under Height and levels are the start height and the level of the first turn. The defaults are the height of the selected track end or the base height of the active level.
    7. With Put each further turn on its own new level (the default) every turn gets its own level: “Helix – turn 2”, “… 3” and so on. This lets you show and hide the turns individually later.
    8. Gradient, total height, footprint, start and end height are shown below the fields. Click Build helix.
    ExampleCurve with a 360 mm radius, height per turn 80 mm. One turn is 2 × 3.14 × 360 ≈ 2260 mm long. 80 mm over 2260 mm is a 3.5 % gradient – already steep. With the next larger radius (about 420 mm) it is only 3.0 %. Larger radii make the helix gentler but need more space.

    The height per turn must be at least the clearance height of the gauge, otherwise no train fits under the next turn. The window points this out.

    Under Construction you choose how the roadbed is supported: on threaded rods (long bolts on which the boards are held with nuts) or in rib construction (vertical boards with slots in which the roadbed boards lie).

    Changing the helix later, inserting a turnout

    The finished helix consists of individual tracks. Each can be changed like any other track. So that the other turns are not in the way, hide all levels in the level bar except the turn you want. To build a turnout into the helix:

    1. Select the track that the turnout is to replace.
    2. In the Properties under Insert turnout, choose the turnout and if needed in the opposite direction and Branch on the other side.
    3. Click Insert turnout. The turnout takes over height, gradient and level. If it is longer than the track, the following tracks are replaced too; a gap is closed with flexible track. Ctrl+Z takes everything back.
    TipIn tight helix curves a curved turnout fits best because it is curved itself. A straight turnout often does not fit there – the program tells you.

    Helix timber list PRO

    After building, the program shows the timber list: everything you need from the DIY store for the helix benchwork, with finished cutting sizes. It can be copied or saved as a CSV file for a spreadsheet.

    PartWhat is it?
    Roadbed segmentsCurved plywood boards (pieces of a circular ring) on which the track lies. With cutting size and inner and outer radius. 4, 6, 8 or 12 per circle, so none is longer than 1200 mm.

    What the program calculates with

    • Track bed: about 2.7 times the track gauge per track; with double track the track spacing is added.
    • Edge: at least 25 mm per side – room for the threaded rod hole (12 mm from the edge) and a guard rail so no train falls off.
    • Saw allowance: 5 mm, already included in every cutting size.
    • Timber thickness: 8 mm for small gauges, 10 mm up to gauge 0, 15 mm for large gauges; base board at least 12 mm.
    • Threaded rods: M8, M10 for large gauges.
    • Board requirement: in boards of 2500 × 1250 mm with 25 % waste.
    TipMark out the segments from the centre with a string and a pencil (like a large compass) and cut them out with a jigsaw. Drill the holes for the threaded rods with a template so that all turns lie exactly on top of each other.

    Cutting template as SVG: this button in the timber list window saves every segment, board and splice plate as a separate drawing at full size (1:1), together in a ZIP file. Printed out, it serves as a template to stick on the wood; it is also suitable for plotters and CNC routers.

    Rib construction for the layout PRO

    In open-frame construction there is no continuous baseboard. Instead, vertical boards called ribs stand on the frame at regular intervals. Narrow roadbed boards lie on them only where tracks run. This makes it easy to form hills and valleys. Tracks › Rib construction… calculates the ribs from the track heights in your plan.

    • Rib direction: across or along the layout, with the maximum spacing in mm.
    • Roadbed width and thickness and the rail top at height 0 above the frame.
    • Each support gets its height and slope from the track heights; beside the supports the top edge slopes away.
    • If one roadbed lies below another, the rib gets a cut-out of clearance height – so the same ribs carry several storeys.

    The result shows each rib with position, length, height, supports and cut-outs. It can be copied, saved as CSV and output as SVG: an overview and each rib as a 1:1 template, together in a ZIP file.

    Roadbed from selection PRO

    For a ramp or a freely routed line, Tracks › Roadbed… calculates the board under the selected tracks:

    1. Select the tracks, for example a ramp.
    2. Click Tracks › Roadbed… and enter roadbed width, thickness, maximum part length and splice plate length.
    3. The roadbed follows the track centre and is divided into parts of equal length with splice plates. Save the parts as SVG: each part as a separate file at full size, with the track joints and their heights drawn in.

    Tunnels

    Select the tracks that are to be in the tunnel and click Tracks › Tunnel (also under Scenery › Tunnel). Tunnel tracks are drawn below the other tracks in the plan, get portals in the 3D view and may lie under a mountain in the plan check. Clicking Tunnel again with tunnel tracks selected turns them back into normal tracks.

    13Wiring, signals and digital

    A locomotive only runs when there is power in the rails. In this chapter you plan where the power comes from, where it gets into the track, where the rails are electrically separated – and let the program check that everything fits together. Signals, digital addresses and feedback sensors follow. You need no previous knowledge; the basics are in the first section.

    The basics in five minutes

    Electricity only flows in a circuit: from the power source through one wire to the locomotive and back through a second wire. On a model railway the rails themselves are the wires. The locomotive picks up the power through its wheels (or a pick-up shoe).

    • Track power (B) is the current that flows to the locomotive. In the program and on many devices the terminal is called “B”; the wire is usually red.
    • Ground (0) is the return path to the power source. The terminal is called “0”; the wire is brown (centre rail) or blue (two-rail). Without ground no current flows – and nothing runs.
    • Feeder: the spot where a wire is connected to the track. This is where power gets into the track.
    • Isolating joint: a spot where the rails are electrically interrupted (with insulated rail joiners instead of metal ones). This creates separate sections that can be supplied or switched off individually.
    • Short circuit: track power and ground touch directly, without a locomotive in between. The power source then shuts down. You want to avoid this – the program warns you.

    Two-rail or centre rail?

    There are two kinds of model railway track. Which one you have follows from the track system you chose.

    Two-rail (DC)Centre rail (AC)
    Track poweron one railon the small studs in the middle of the track
    Groundon the other railon both rails
    Wire coloursred and bluered and brown
    What to watchWithin a section, track power must always be on the same rail. A reversing loop needs special wiring. Polarity and reversing loops do not matter.

    Analog or digital?

    • Analog: a power pack (throttle) puts power into the track. The more power, the faster the locomotive runs. All locomotives in the same section run at the same time.
    • Digital: a command station keeps power in the track all the time and sends commands with it. Each locomotive has its own address and obeys only its own commands. Large layouts add boosters – amplifiers that supply further sections with power.
    • An accessory transformer supplies lights, turnouts and signals. It does not belong on the track.

    Step by step: power to the track

    This is how to supply a simple layout with power. The example is an oval with a command station.

    1. Set operation and current

    Click Wiring › Operation / current. Choose Digital or Analog and DC (two-rail) or AC (centre rail). If in doubt, leave the current at Automatic (from track) – it then follows your track system. The check, the polarity and the suggested wire colours depend on it.

    2. Place the command station or transformer

    Click Wiring › Transformer / command station and choose what to place – in digital operation a command station or booster, in analog operation a power pack, and in both an accessory transformer. Then click in the plan. The device appears as a box with two terminals: B (track power) and 0 (ground). You may also put it next to the baseboard, for example where it will really stand.

    3. Lay the track power wire

    1. Click the command station (or transformer).
    2. Click it with the right mouse button and choose Lay wire from here (track power). The same button is also in the properties on the right.
    3. The wire starts at terminal B. Click points in the plan along which the wire is to run – for example along the underside of the baseboard.
    4. Finally click on a track and finish with a double-click (in the web version also with Done).

    Where the wire ends on the track, a feeder is created. It is shown in the plan as a small symbol at the track.

    4. Lay the ground wire

    1. Right-click the command station and choose Lay ground wire from here (0).
    2. Click points and let the wire end on a track – with two-rail track on the other rail from the track power. A double-click finishes.

    A ground connection is created there. With centre rail it does not matter which rail; with two-rail the program makes sure track power and ground are on different rails.

    Example: How many feeders?Power loses strength over long distances because rail joiners do not conduct perfectly. As a rule of thumb there should be a feeder about every 2 to 3 metres. An oval with 6 m of track therefore gets two or three track power wires. Simply choose Lay wire from here several times; a track may also have several feeders. The check gives a note if there are too few.

    Changing or deleting a feeder

    • Click a feeder. The properties show which source it is connected to.
    • On the right rail (in track direction) moves it to the other rail. You need this with two-rail track when the check reports “wrong rail”.
    • Del deletes the feeder. If the wire is deleted, its feeder disappears too.

    Isolating joints, circuits and reversing loops

    Placing an isolating joint

    Switch on Wiring › Isolating joint and click the spot where two tracks meet. An isolating joint is created there; a second click removes it again. Alternatively: right-click a track and choose Isolations (Windows: Isolators and feedback sensors) – there you switch the isolation on at each end individually. With two-rail track an isolating joint means “both rails insulated”, with centre rail “centre rail separated”.

    What for? For example for a siding where a locomotive is to stand without power, for signals that switch off the power in front of them, for the boundary between command station and booster, or for a reversing loop.

    Showing circuits

    Wiring › Show circuits colours each section in its own colour. You see at once which tracks are electrically connected. Label electrics only hides heights and article numbers and writes the section numbers on the tracks instead.

    Reversing loop

    A reversing loop is a track arrangement in which a train turns round and comes back in the opposite direction – for example a loop at the end of a line. With two-rail track the left rail meets the right one: track power meets ground, a short circuit. The solution: put isolating joints at both ends of the loop and supply it via a reversing module (digital) or a polarity reversing switch (analog). The program detects reversing loops itself and reports them. With centre rail this problem does not exist.

    Wire automatically

    The quickest way to finished wiring: build the track plan, set the isolating joints, then Electrics › Wire automatically (also in the context menu). The program adds feeds with track power and ground to every section – several on long sections, spaced by the feeder rule – and runs the wires to the source.

    • Small layout on one level: the command station feeds all sections.
    • Several levels (or a lot of track): one booster per level or per started 25 m of track (H0); the command station is only used for switching. For a level to get its own booster it must be a separate section, divided off by isolating joints.
    • DC (two-rail): every reversing loop is detected, missing isolating joints at its ends are set and the loop is fed via a reversing module KS1, KS2 …. The module gets track power (B) and ground (0) at the top from the command station or booster.
    • Sections with a detector (digital) run via the occupancy detector; it hangs on the command station or a booster like a booster does. You can also place the occupancy detector yourself via Electrics › Occupancy detector.

    Running it again replaces the automatically added wires, boosters and modules; wires you laid yourself stay. Everything can be undone in one step.

    Checking the wiring

    Wiring › Check wiring examines the whole plan and lists everything unusual. Clicking a message shows the spot in the plan. Errors must be fixed, otherwise the layout will not run or there will be a short circuit. Warnings should be looked at. Notes are advice.

    Digital addresses

    In digital operation every turnout and every signal gets an address – a number by which the command station speaks to it, like a house number. Wiring › Addresses shows all turnouts and signals in a list.

    • Enter addresses one by one, or have them assigned in sequence with Assign from, starting from a number of your choice. With only missing, existing addresses are kept.
    • Turnouts use one address, double slips and main signals two.
    • Duplicate addresses are marked in red. You can also give each entry a name, for example “T3 station entry”.

    Feedback sensors

    Feedback sensors tell a digital control system (or control software on a computer) where a train is. Choose Wiring › Detector and the type:

    • Occupancy detector – notices from the current drawn by vehicles that a track section is occupied. With two-rail track one rail is separated for this.
    • Contact track – reports when a train passes a particular spot.
    • Reed contact – a small switch between the rails, triggered by a magnet under the vehicle.

    Then click the tracks. The contacts are numbered in sequence, 16 per feedback module. Clicking an existing sensor removes it. Change name, type and contact number in the properties. The sensors are numbered “Melder 1”, “Melder 2” and so on.

    In digital operation an occupancy detector sits between the command station or booster and the track: the traction power flows through the detector into the track. In the properties, choose under Traction power from (command station / booster) where the detector gets its power from. A new detector automatically gets the command station or booster that already feeds its section, otherwise the first booster or the command station in the plan. The section then counts as fed – Check electrics no longer reports a missing feeder there. A red dashed line in the plan shows the connection, and the wiring plan lists “Traction power through Melder 3”.

    Electrics › Wire detectors (also in the context menu: Connect detectors to module) connects every feedback sensor that has a contact number to the matching terminal of its feedback module with a violet wire. Contact 5 goes to input 5 of the first module, contact 20 to input 4 of the second. If there is no module for a contact yet, the program places a generic occupancy detector below the track plan (“Besetztmelder 1”, “Besetztmelder 2” …, one per 16 contacts). Track power (B, red) and ground (0, brown) are at the top of the occupancy detector: from there one wire each runs to the command station or booster you chose for the sensor under “Traction power from”. Feed and ground of the section are then fine. While the function is on, the wires follow every change: a new sensor, another contact number, a moved track or a deleted sensor. Clicking Wire detectors again (Remove detector wires) takes all these wires out again; the automatically added occupancy detectors go with them, feedback modules you placed yourself stay.

    Numbering

    Wiring › Number gives all feeders (G1, G2 …), feedback sensors (Melder 1, Melder 2 …) and wires (K1, K2 …) a number, from top left to bottom right. Write the same numbers on the real wires when building – then you will find every wire again later.

    Wiring plan

    Wiring › Wiring plan sums up all the wiring as a list – to print and take under the layout:

    • power supply: every command station, booster and transformer with the number of its feeders and whether ground is connected,
    • all sections with length and their feeders including the rail (left/right),
    • all isolating joints, all feedback sensors with module and input,
    • all wires with use, colour, cross-section, length, start and end,
    • a shopping list of wire per colour and cross-section, with 15 % extra,
    • the reversing loops.

    The wiring plan can be saved as a text file in the Pro edition.

    Routes

    A route is a path from one signal to the next, with all turnouts in between in the right position. Wiring › Routes finds all such paths – up to the next signal in the direction of travel, a buffer stop or an open track end. Each route shows the turnouts with their position (straight, diverging, left, right), the length and the addresses. Routes that need the same turnout in different positions are marked as a conflict. This function is part of the Basic edition.

    Export for control software PRO

    If you control your layout with software on a computer, you would have to enter all turnouts, signals and addresses there again.     19Questions and answers

    A track does not snap into place.

    Check that snapping is switched on: Snap at the bottom of the status bar (in the web version also under View). Tracks only snap to free ends; if the end is already used, choose the next open end with E.

    An oval does not quite close.

    Small gaps appear when straights of different lengths are mixed. Close the gap with Tracks › Close gap or draw flexible track (chapter 8).

    I have changed too much.

    Ctrl+Z takes back the last step. History… shows the last steps; a click jumps to an earlier state and forward again. On Windows the history is on the Home tab, in the web version it is an icon at the top of the title bar next to undo and redo.

    Tracks have suddenly disappeared.

    Their level is probably hidden. Tick it again in the level bar at the bottom, click Show all or choose Show all in the Levels window. If Show scenery is off, hills, water, buildings and trees are missing as well.

    A track cannot be moved, rotated or deleted.

    It is locked. Select it and click Home › Lock, or untick locked in the properties.

    One click suddenly selects many tracks.

    The tracks are grouped. Home › Ungroup separates them again.

    “Section … has no feeder”.

    No track power wire leads into this track section yet. Often there is an isolating joint in between that you forgot. Switch on Wiring › Show circuits to see the sections, and lay a wire from the command station to a track in this section with Lay wire from here (chapter 13).

    “Ground missing” or “no return”.

    The return path of the current is missing. Right-click the command station or transformer, choose Lay ground wire from here (0) and let the wire end on a rail – with two-rail track on the other rail from the track power.

    “Feeder on the wrong rail”.

    With two-rail track, track power is on the rail that already carries ground in this section. Click the feeder and switch On the right rail (in track direction).

    A note about the Pro edition appears when saving.

    The Basic edition saves plans with up to 50 tracks. For larger plans install your licence under File › Licence… (chapter 3).

    The licence is reported as invalid.

    The licence file must not be changed – not even by opening and saving it in a text editor. If the licence is bound to a computer, it only applies there; if an expiry date is set, only up to that day. Contact the publisher with your computer ID.

    The licence is valid but “not activated on this device”.

    The licence is already running on two other devices, or the device has not been connected to the internet for 14 days. Release another device or connect to the internet and click Activate now in the licence window (section Online activation).

    Windows reports “Windows protected your PC” during installation.

    Click More info and then Run anyway. The message appears for programs that Windows has not seen often yet.

    Android does not install the app.

    Allow the app you use to open the file (for example “Files” or the browser) to install unknown apps. If an older version with a different signature was installed, uninstall it first.

    The background image does not match my dimensions.

    Draw a line over a known distance in the image with Home › Dimension, select it and call Room › Scale to measurement… (chapter 12).

    The plan check says the door cannot open.

    The door's swing area lies under the baseboard. Move or rotate the door, or make the baseboard smaller at that point.

    How much does my layout cost?

    Open the parts list (Pro), enter stock and prices or import a price list. The total is at the bottom (chapter 16).

    The 3D view stays black or is jerky.

    Update the graphics driver or the browser. On very large layouts Track plan only helps.

    Where is my data?

    Plans are wherever you saved them. On Windows the licence, settings and automatic backup are under %APPDATA%\TrackMaster Pro; the web version stores settings and your own models in the browser. The program does not send plans to third parties; at start-up it only retrieves the current version number.

    20Glossary

    TermMeaning
    Accessory transformerTransformer for lights, turnouts and signals; does not belong on the track.
    Active track endThe orange-marked free end where the next track is added.
    AddressNumber by which a command station speaks to a turnout, signal or locomotive.
    Analog operationA power pack controls the voltage in the track; all locomotives in the section run at the same time.
    BaseboardThe surface the layout is built on, usually a wooden board on a frame.
    BoosterAmplifier in digital operation that supplies another section with power. An isolating joint belongs between booster and command station sections.
    Buffer stopEnd of a track where the train stops.
    Centre railKind of track with studs in the middle of the track for the track power; both rails are ground. Operated with AC.
    Clearance heightMinimum height between two tracks on top of each other so that vehicles fit underneath; depends on the gauge.
    Command stationHeart of digital operation: produces power and commands for locomotives, turnouts and signals.
    ConnectorOne end of a track piece; a turnout has three, a crossing four.
    CSV fileSimple table file that any spreadsheet can open.
    CurveCurved track piece with a fixed radius and angle, for example 30°. Twelve 30° curves make a circle.
    Digital operationA command station keeps power in the track and sends commands with it; each locomotive listens only to its address.
    DistributorTerminal strip where many wires are joined, for example the common ground.
    DXF fileDrawing format for CAD programs, CNC routers and laser cutters.
    Feedback sensorTells the digital control whether a track is occupied or a train passes a spot.
    FeederSpot where a wire from command station, booster or transformer ends on the track and brings power into it. Created with “Lay wire from here”.
    Fiddle yardHidden storage yard, usually under the layout, where trains wait.
    Flexible trackTrack in lengths that can be bent and cut as required.
    GaugeSize of the model railway, for example H0, N or Z; determines track gauge and scale.
    GradientHow steeply a track rises, in percent: 2 % means 2 cm of height per 1 m of line.
    Ground (0)Return path of the current to the power source; terminal “0”, wire brown (centre rail) or blue (two-rail).
    GroupCombined tracks that are selected and moved together with one click.
    HelixSpiral rising line to climb large heights in little space.
    Isolating jointInsulated rail joint that separates two power sections.
    Level“Storey” of the layout with its own base height that can be shown and hidden.
    ModuleRectangular layout part that can be combined with others to form a layout.
    Open track endEnd of a track piece without a connection; marked orange in the plan.
    PropertiesArea on the right of the window; shows and changes the values of the selected track or object.
    RadiusDistance from the centre of the circle to the middle of a curved track; the smaller, the tighter.
    Rail topReference height for all height values in the plan.
    Reversing loopTrack arrangement in which a train turns and comes back in the opposite direction; with two-rail track it needs special wiring, otherwise there is a short circuit.
    RibVertical board on the frame on which the roadbed rests in open-frame construction.
    RibbonBar at the top of the window with all commands, arranged in tabs.
    RoadbedBoard under the track that carries it at the desired height.
    RoutePath from one signal to the next with all turnouts in the right position.
    ScaleRatio between model and prototype; in H0, 1 cm on the model is 87 cm in reality (1:87).
    Section (power section)Tracks that are electrically connected. Sections are separated from each other by isolating joints.
    Short circuitTrack power and ground touch directly; the power source shuts down.
    Side rail, cross memberOuter frame and cross bracing of the benchwork.
    SidingTrack ending at a buffer stop where trains or wagons are parked.
    Stop aspectSignal indication meaning “stop”.
    TabA tab on the ribbon (Home, Tracks, Scenery …); a click shows its commands.
    Track power (B)The current that flows to the locomotive; terminal “B”, wire usually red.
    Track systemA manufacturer's track range; its pieces fit together exactly.
    TurnoutTrack piece where a track divides into two (or three) directions.
    Two-railKind of track in which the two rails carry track power and ground. Operated with DC.
    Usable lengthThe maximum length of a train on a track.
